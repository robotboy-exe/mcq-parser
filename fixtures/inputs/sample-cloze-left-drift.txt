PASSAGE III
The development of education in many African societies has been influenced by traditional beliefs, economic circumstances and the availability of educational facilities. Before the introduction of formal Western education, knowledge was passed from one generation to another through observation, storytelling, apprenticeship and participation in communal activities. Children learnt the customs, occupations and values of their communities by watching and assisting older members of the family.

With the arrival of missionary organisations, formal schools gradually became established in different parts of the continent. These schools introduced reading, writing and arithmetic, alongside religious instruction. Although many communities welcomed the new system, others were initially suspicious of it because they feared that Western education might undermine their cultural practices. Nevertheless, the advantages of literacy soon became apparent, particularly in trade, administration and communication.

Today, education is widely regarded as an important instrument of national development. It provides individuals with the knowledge and skills required to participate effectively in society. However, access to quality education remains uneven. Children in rural communities often face challenges such as inadequate classrooms, insufficient teaching materials and a shortage of qualified teachers. In some urban areas, overcrowded classrooms and the high cost of private education also limit learning opportunities.

Governments and private organisations have made efforts to address these problems through the construction of schools, the provision of scholarships and the training of teachers. However, such efforts can only produce lasting results when educational policies are properly implemented and adequate funds are made available. It is therefore necessary for all stakeholders, including parents, teachers and community leaders, to contribute to the improvement of educational standards.
